Independent analysis · Dangote Sugar Refinery Plc

It earns ₦123bn refining sugar and pays ₦175bn to finance the raw material.

Backward integration at Dangote Sugar is usually described as a growth project. The FY2025 accounts say it is something else: the only available repair to a balance sheet that is refinanced every twelve months.

Nigeria grows two per cent of the sugar it eats

The country consumes about 1.53 million tonnes a year and produces roughly two per cent of it. Essentially the whole remainder arrives from a single country, priced in dollars and financed on short-dated letters of credit.

Phase 2 of the plan, running to 2033, allocates raw sugar import quota on backward integration performance rather than refining capacity. Progress on the estates now determines access to the raw material the refinery runs on. A delay has a regulatory consequence as well as an economic one.

What a tonne of cane actually removes

The programme is normally framed as replacing an import with domestic production. It does three things at once, and only the first is a production cost.

How the model was built

The central move is to stop reading the company's volumes and realised price as given — neither is disclosed and instead derive both from the audited cost of sales, so the unit economics reconcile to the accounts by construction rather than by assumption.

Structure

Twelve tabs, 1,119 formulas, 31 automated integrity checks, FY2024 to FY2032 so the cane ramp is visible. The estates are modelled as a standalone project against the counterfactual of continuing to import every tonne, so the programme is valued on the difference rather than on a growth story.

How volume and price were derived

Dangote Sugar does not disclose sales volume or realised price. Cost of sales is disclosed - revenue less gross profit - so volume falls out of it once the cash cost per tonne refined is built from the world raw sugar price, freight and duty, refining loss and conversion cost. FY2025 volume comes to 0.863 Mt, which is 60 per cent of the 1.44 Mt Apapa capacity and a credible number. Realised price is then revenue over that volume. Gross margin reconciles to the audited 14.8 per cent by construction.

The assumption doing the most work

The blended cost of financing, 13.7 per cent, is not assumed. It is calibrated so the model reproduces the disclosed ₦175.3bn finance charge from the sum of borrowings and the naira value of raw sugar under letters of credit. An automated check fails if that calibration ever drifts. The estate cash cost of $320 a tonne is the genuinely soft number, and the sensitivity ranking shows it third.

Why two different return figures appear

The project internal rate of return is taken on a series whose final term is a perpetuity already discounted at 18 per cent, and the 5,000 hectares standing at the start of the model carry no capex in it. Both flatter the figure. The implied perpetual return on developed land - cash from one hectare against the capex on that hectare, with no terminal value assumption and no free acreage - is the cleaner measure, and it is 11.7 per cent. Both are shown rather than the more favourable one being chosen.

What is not modelled

The regulatory quota is treated qualitatively rather than as a modelled revenue stream, because the allocation formula under Phase 2 is not public in enough detail to price. The company does not split interest from exchange loss within finance charges, so neither does the model. Administrative and selling costs are held as a single ₦19.61bn residual implied by the FY2025 bridge rather than projected line by line.
